\title{Cache-Friendly by Design: The Optimizations Inside cMVBT-OSIC}
\author{Amir Tonta}
\date{August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
This document explains, layer by layer, how \texttt{cMVBT-OSIC} is built to keep a
CPU's caches, TLB, and lock-free fast paths working \emph{for} it rather than against
it. It is organized by architectural layer --- physical page layout, then
concurrency control built on top of that layout, then structural maintenance, then
the transaction layer, then garbage collection, then the cross-cutting sharding and
snapshot-caching machinery, then durability --- because that is the order in which
each optimization actually depends on the one before it, not the order in which any
of it was written. A short section up front (\S\ref{sec:foundational}) argues for
four specific decisions that are \emph{foundational}: nearly everything described
later either depends on one of them for correctness, or depends on one of them for
the cost it removes to exist in the first place. Wherever the implementation
contains a controlled before/after measurement, that number is reported with its
workload and caveats attached; wherever it was practical to do so, those numbers were
independently re-measured while writing this document, and both are reported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: cache-friendliness as the throughline}
\label{sec:intro}

Almost every optimization in this system reduces to one of a small number of
recurring moves: touch fewer cache lines to decide the same thing, avoid an
allocation on a path that runs millions of times a second, let a lock-free reader
skip a lock entirely, or shard a piece of shared bookkeeping so unrelated threads
stop invalidating each other's cache lines. This document is organized so those moves
are visible as patterns, not as a list of unrelated tweaks.

\paragraph{Roadmap.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Foundational optimizations} (\S\ref{sec:foundational}): the four
        decisions everything else either depends on or is a response to.
  \item \textbf{Physical layer} (\S\ref{sec:physical}): how one page holds several
        versions of a key, laid out to minimize cache and TLB pressure.
  \item \textbf{Concurrency control} (\S\ref{sec:cc}): optimistic lock coupling and
        the latch every node uses.
  \item \textbf{Structural maintenance} (\S\ref{sec:smo}): splitting and merging
        pages as they fill up or empty out.
  \item \textbf{The transaction layer} (\S\ref{sec:tx}): bounding physical growth,
        caching resolved roots across repeated reads, reverse-order batched abort,
        and the auto-commit fast path for single-key operations.
  \item \textbf{Garbage collection} (\S\ref{sec:gc}) and \textbf{sharding}
        (\S\ref{sec:sharding}): reclaiming retired pages, and the recurring pattern
        of partitioning shared bookkeeping by worker or by table.
  \item \textbf{Per-thread identity and the snapshot cache} (\S\ref{sec:snapcache}):
        how a worker remembers who it is and memoizes visibility decisions across
        calls, in detail.
  \item \textbf{OSIC and the write-ahead log} (\S\ref{sec:osic}): decoupling
        visibility from durability, and the write-path optimizations that follow
        from it.
  \item \textbf{Range scans} (\S\ref{sec:scans}), \textbf{workload/benchmark-harness
        optimizations} (\S\ref{sec:workload}), and a \textbf{case study}
        (\S\ref{sec:bigtree}) on trading contention for scan cost.
  \item \textbf{Fresh measurements} (\S\ref{sec:fresh}), a \textbf{combined gains
        table} (\S\ref{sec:combined}), and the \textbf{conclusion}
        (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Evidence levels.} Every claimed gain is tagged \gainmeasured{} (a
before/after benchmark exists, with its workload and caveats), \gainbounded{} (a
countable cost --- an allocation, a lock, a cache line, a retry --- is removably
bounded, but no end-to-end percentage was recorded), or \gainenabling{} (the change
is primarily structural: it removes a class of cost or a piece of shared state
rather than claiming a specific speedup). Combined benchmark results are never
attributed to one component in isolation unless a measurement exists that isolates
it.

\section{Foundational optimizations: what everything else stands on}
\label{sec:foundational}

Four design decisions sit underneath nearly everything described later, in the
specific sense that removing any one of them would not just cost throughput --- it
would make a later optimization either unsound or pointless.

\subsection{Append-only, publish-once physical pages}

Every leaf page is a fixed-capacity array that is only ever appended to. An
obsolete, aborted, or deleted entry is \emph{marked} dead; it is never physically
overwritten or removed until the next structural-maintenance pass rebuilds the page
from scratch (\S\ref{sec:physical}). Internal pages follow the same rule: a
superseded child entry is never mutated, only shadowed by a newer appended entry
(\S\ref{sec:physical}, \S\ref{sec:gc}).

\textbf{What this underwrites.} Optimistic lock coupling (\S\ref{sec:cc}) lets
readers inspect a page with no latch at all specifically because nothing they might
observe was ever mutated after being published. The internal-page live mask
(\S\ref{sec:physical}) is a pure function of \emph{disjoint, append-ordered
intervals} --- it has no meaning if slots could be mutated in place. And the
version-growth bound in \S\ref{sec:tx} rests on the notion of ``this transaction's
own, exclusively owned tuple,'' which only exists because writes append rather than
clobber.

\subsection{Optimistic lock coupling and the SmartCell latch}

Readers take an atomic version sample of a node's latch and validate it after use,
with no lock acquired at all; writers upgrade to an exclusive, seqlock-style write
lock only on the node they actually mutate (\S\ref{sec:cc}).

\textbf{What this underwrites.} Every sharding optimization in \S\ref{sec:sharding}
--- per-worker bookkeeping slots, the garbage collector's per-shard dead-block
index, cache-padding those slots against false sharing --- is a fix for contention
\emph{among concurrent, lock-free readers and writers}. If reads instead took a page
latch, most of that contention would already be serialized by the latch, and
sharding the bookkeeping around it would buy nothing.

\subsection{OSIC: decoupling visibility from durability}

Every transaction draws a start and a commit timestamp from one global counter; a
write becomes visible to other workers the instant its commit timestamp lands in
the committing worker's append-only commit log --- an in-memory step with no wait on
disk I/O (\S\ref{sec:osic}).

\textbf{What this underwrites.} The whole write-ahead-log performance story in
\S\ref{sec:osic} --- sharding the write channel, a lock-free alternative backend,
exact framing sizes --- is optimizing the cost of \emph{catching up durability
asynchronously}. If commit instead required a synchronous disk round-trip, none of
those micro-optimizations would matter: the round-trip's latency would dwarf all of
them by one to two orders of magnitude.

\subsection{A per-worker identity substrate}

A worker identity is assigned once per (tree, thread) and cached thread-locally
(\S\ref{sec:snapcache}); it is the index into every per-worker structure the rest of
the system builds (\S\ref{sec:sharding}).

\textbf{What this underwrites.} Sharding, as a pattern, needs something to shard
\emph{along}. The garbage collector's dead-block tracker, the transaction context's
live-snapshot slots, and the thread-local snapshot cache are all indexed the same
way. Without a stable per-worker identity assigned once and reused, each of those
would need its own separate discovery mechanism.

\subsection{A related, second-order optimization: bounding version growth}

One further optimization is worth flagging here even though it depends on the four
foundations above rather than being depended on by everything: without the
version-growth bound described in \S\ref{sec:tx}, the structural-maintenance tuning
in \S\ref{sec:smo} --- capacity thresholds, split-boundary search, merge-candidate
selection --- would be fighting an unbounded adversary, since a single transaction
that writes one key many times inside itself can otherwise leave a page with no
valid split boundary at all.

\section{Physical layer: pages, records, and memory layout}
\label{sec:physical}

\subsection{Append-only leaves and the version tuple}

A leaf's record array is append-only, as established above; a packed length counter
tracks active vs.\ dead slots. Each tuple carries a version header: an insertion
stamp (a packed \code{(worker\_id, ts\_start)}, constant for the whole transaction,
not per write) and an optional deletion stamp. Lookups scan \emph{backward} for the
newest live entry, since physical append order places the newest write last ---
this is exactly why point lookup is a reverse linear scan rather than a sorted
search: the leaf is not key-sorted, and append order is the only order it actually
guarantees.

\subsection{Structure-of-arrays leaf layout}

A leaf's physical layout is a dense key region followed by a parallel
version/payload region, not one array of complete \code{\{key, version, payload\}}
records:

\begin{lstlisting}
key_region:   [key0, key1, key2, ...]
data_region:  [(version0, payload0), (version1, payload1), ...]
\end{lstlisting}

Point lookup walks the key region backward; version metadata and payload cache
lines are touched only \emph{after} a key matches, and a range scan can test its key
range before loading the parallel data at all. This is a pure locality change: for
\code{u64} keys and a one-word payload slot, one physical entry is still 32 bytes
(8 bytes key, 24 bytes version+payload), so capacity is unaffected --- only how many
unrelated cache lines a rejected key touches on the way to being rejected.

\subsection{The leaf's live/validity mask}

A normal leaf has at most 123 physical slots, so two 64-bit words (128 bits) cover
every slot's liveness directly inside the page, next to the key/data regions
themselves, rather than behind a separate boxed-slice handle and its pointer chase.
A clear bit means the slot has been invalidated (by an abort or a compaction) and
can be skipped by lookup \emph{before} its version header and payload are ever
touched --- the same ``reject cheaply, load lazily'' principle as the
structure-of-arrays layout above, taken one level further: instead of loading a key
to reject a slot, a lookup can reject a whole slot with one bit test against a mask
that already lives in the leaf's own first cache line. This is a
\emph{physical}-liveness mask, distinct from MVCC visibility (which also depends on
the reading transaction's snapshot and cannot be represented by one bit per slot),
but it composes with the version check that follows it: a lookup pays for the
version/visibility check only on slots this mask has not already ruled out.
Deliberately oversized leaf variants (\S\ref{sec:bigtree}) fall back to a heap
bitmap through a same-sized union representation, so the normal leaf's mask never
grows past two words no matter how large a specialized leaf variant's capacity gets.

\subsection{The internal page's live mask}

Internal pages are append-only across structural versions: replacing a child
appends one or more new key-interval entries while the old interval stays
physically present for older snapshots to route through. Deciding which entries are
\emph{currently} live --- the set a fresh, non-historical traversal or a structural
rebuild should actually consider --- would naively require rebuilding a temporary
liveness vector from every pair of entries' intervals each time it is needed, an
$O(\mathrm{FAN\_OUT}^2)$ reconstruction paid again and again on the structural-maintenance
cold path.

The internal page instead carries its own live mask, maintained incrementally
\emph{at append time}: appending a new child interval clears the mask bit of every
older entry that interval overlaps, sets the new entry's own bit, and publishes the
new physical length as usual. This exploits an invariant that holds throughout the
tree's life --- live sibling key intervals are always pairwise disjoint --- so ``is
entry $i$ still part of the current partition'' reduces to one word load and one bit
test, and counting the live entries reduces to a popcount over at most two words.
Every later consumer of this information --- merge-candidate selection, survivor
counting, split copying --- reads the mask directly instead of rebuilding it, moving
the $O(\mathrm{FAN\_OUT}^2)$ cost from ``every time someone asks'' to ``once, when
the page's contents actually change.''

This mask is deliberately \emph{not} consulted by historical routing: a child
superseded in the current partition may still be the correct route for an older
snapshot, so historical reads keep selecting via each entry's own immutable version,
independent of this mask entirely. The mask is a current-state acceleration
structure layered on top of the version metadata, not a replacement for it.

\subsection{Exact page-sized allocation, payload indirection, and the hot/cold split}

The 4\,KiB target applies to the full allocated cell, including the concurrency
latch's own version word and node metadata --- not just the record array. With
\code{FAN\_OUT = NUM\_RECORDS = 123}, the cell lands at exactly 4096 bytes; one
additional record per array would cross that boundary. This two-record trim is
\gainmeasured{}: page-aligned allocations rose from 25\% to 100\% of the time, with a
9--12\% reduction in dTLB load misses --- concrete evidence that ``nominally full''
and ``lands cleanly on a hardware page'' are two different numbers, and only the
second one is free.

A one-word payload slot stores a \code{u64} or a thin row handle inline; larger rows
go through a reference-counted pointer instead, so cloning a read result or copying
a survivor during a split increments a refcount rather than copying a whole row.
The tree's own top-level handle is split into a hot part --- root, allocator,
transaction context, everything the traversal-critical path touches --- and a
separately boxed cold part holding construction-time configuration (WAL handle,
table id, capacity bounds) that the hot path never needs to read, purely to shrink
the cache footprint of the part that does.

\subsection{Same-allocation reuse across page types}

A node's leaf and internal representations occupy one \code{union}: switching a
node from one page type to the other (as structural maintenance sometimes requires)
drops the previously active variant and constructs the newly needed one in the same
allocation, rather than freeing one page object and allocating a fresh one of the
other type. The allocator only ever sees a churn of same-sized objects, never a
mixed-size alternation between two different page-type footprints.

\subsection{Measured: leaf layout, isolated}

The structure-of-arrays layout's benefit was isolated in a standalone,
dependency-free microbenchmark comparing it against the earlier array-of-structures
layout, with no tree traversal, latching, transactions, or allocation in the timed
region: 16,384 independently boxed leaf pages per layout (a 62.5\,MiB working set
each), five append-ordered update waves per key, point lookups walking backward,
scans checking the range before visibility.

\section{Concurrency control: optimistic lock coupling}
\label{sec:cc}

\subsection{The SmartCell latch}

Every node's latch is a single 64-bit versioned word with three logical bit ranges
packed into it: a seqlock-style write flag, pin-related flags, and a permanent
retired tombstone bit. An optimistic read is one atomic load with the write/retired
bits masked out --- no lock taken, no cache line written by a reader at all.
Upgrading to exclusive is a compare-and-swap from the captured read version to the
write-flagged version, failing immediately if the retired bit is already set. Unlock
stores the incremented version with the retired bit preserved exactly as it was
found, so a same-critical-section retirement is never clobbered by the unlock itself.
A guard that will unconditionally be consumed with no back-out path can retire
directly from a reader snapshot with a single compare-and-swap, skipping the
intermediate exclusive-lock step entirely --- one fewer lock acquisition on the
structural-maintenance path for exactly the guards that never need to back out.

\subsection{Only the node being mutated is ever latched}

Readers are fully lock-free: they sample a node's version, read its (immutable, or
atomically published) contents, and validate the sampled version before relying on
the result, retrying if it changed. Writers acquire an exclusive guard only on the
node they are actually about to mutate --- there is no global traversal guard, and
no hand-over-hand latching down the tree. This is the property the whole sharding
story in \S\ref{sec:sharding} exists to exploit: contention among lock-free
participants is a real cost worth removing, in a way it would not be if a coarser
lock already serialized most of it.

\subsection{Removing fences that atomics already subsume}

Once a data-race-free protocol's own atomic loads/stores are trusted to carry the
ordering a memory fence would otherwise provide, a standalone fence next to that
atomic op is pure overhead. Several such fences, added early out of caution, were
identified as redundant once the protocol's actual happens-before edges were traced
precisely: a length publish already carries the ordering a paired fence next to it
would duplicate; a page's soft-commit counter can use a relaxed store because the
write-unlock that follows it always publishes with an acquire/release pair of its
own; a node's type tag, mutated only under the exclusive write lock, needs no fence
on drop because the latch's own release already publishes it. The general pattern:
every real publication edge in this system is exactly one atomic operation wide, and
a fence is only ever needed where no atomic operation already sits on that edge ---
which, once the protocol is fully traced, turns out to be almost nowhere on the
per-record hot path.

\section{Structural maintenance: choosing where to split and what to merge}
\label{sec:smo}

Splitting and merging pages is the cold path that keeps every page's capacity
bounded; the algorithms below decide \emph{where} to cut and \emph{which} neighbor
to combine with, so that the common case needs no retry.

\subsection{Capacity and overflow thresholds}

An internal page is allowed to fill much closer to its true capacity before a
split is attempted at all, rather than reserving several slots of headroom
defensively --- headroom that is rarely needed and, when it is not, simply forces
more structural work than the page's real capacity requires.

\subsection{Split: a tiered boundary search}

Splitting a leaf chooses between compacting dead versions out of the page in place
(when that alone brings it back under capacity) and cutting it into two pages at a
key boundary. Choosing that boundary is a search with two competing goals: prefer a
boundary that does not cut across one key's own version chain, and prefer a
boundary whose two halves both fit within capacity. Rather than combining both goals
into one greedy pass --- which can miss a boundary that satisfies both simply
because it lies farther from the naive midpoint than the search first looks ---
the search tries a small number of increasingly permissive tiers in order: first, a
boundary that satisfies both goals; failing that, one that fits capacity but may cut
a version chain; failing that, one that avoids cutting a chain but may leave one side
over capacity; and only as a last resort, the raw midpoint. This tiered search costs
a few extra integer comparisons in the rare case where the ideal boundary is not the
first one tried, in exchange for finding a boundary that satisfies both goals
whenever one genuinely exists.

\subsection{Merge: emptier-sibling selection and survivor accounting}

Merging considers \emph{both} adjacent siblings and picks whichever has more
available room, rather than always defaulting to one side --- a page with a nearly
full sibling on one side and a nearly empty one on the other should combine with the
empty one. Deciding whether a merge will actually fit requires knowing how many
records will genuinely survive the combination, not merely how many are currently
counted \emph{active}: a record whose deleting transaction is still unresolved must
be retained until that transaction's fate is known (it may still abort and need its
predecessor back), so an active-count estimate alone can understate the true
survivor count and risk overflowing the fixed-capacity destination page. The merge
path counts true survivors before committing to the merge, falling back to a key
split instead whenever the honest count would not fit --- the same preflight
philosophy as the leaf's live/validity mask: decide cheaply and correctly before
doing the expensive copy, not after.

When combining two leaves' records into one, physical append order is preserved
rather than re-sorted by any transaction timestamp: the newest-version lookup
described in \S\ref{sec:physical} depends on physical append order tracking
recency, and re-sorting by an unrelated key would silently break that invariant for
every future lookup on the combined page.

\section{Root indexing strategies}
\label{sec:root}

Before traversing anything, a reader has to find ``the root as of my snapshot'' ---
a lookup keyed by version into a table's own root-version index. Four
interchangeable structures are available for this, each trading lookup complexity
against per-append cost and memory overhead differently:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.6cm}p{4.4cm}p{2.9cm}p{4.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{Structure} & \textbf{Lookup} & \textbf{Notes} \\
\midrule
Vanilla & Linked list of roots & $O(n)$ linear & Simplest baseline. \\
Skip-list & Lock-free ordered map & $O(\log n)$ expected & Concurrent inserts and
  lookups with no shared lock. \\
B-tree-indexed & An external, latch-coupled B+-tree keyed by version & $O(\log n)$
  & Reuses a full concurrent index just to look up root pointers by version. \\
Frugal (default) & A lock-free singly-linked list with a probabilistic skip
  tower & Sub-linear expected, flat-chain worst case & Appending is a single
  pointer store with no retry loop needed, since appends are already serialized by
  the writer that is publishing the new root; a much smaller per-entry footprint
  than a full skip-list tower. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each of these is one single, tree-wide structure holding every historical root --- it
is not partitioned the way \S\ref{sec:sharding}'s sharding pattern partitions other
state, because the thing being optimized here is different: the cost of resolving
one specific version to one specific root pointer, not contention among many workers
touching unrelated pieces of shared bookkeeping.

\section{The transaction layer}
\label{sec:tx}

\subsection{Bounding version growth: self-overwrite and the delete/reinsert cycle}
\label{sec:selfoverwrite}

This is one of the more interesting problems this system solves, and it is worth
walking through carefully, because the fix is small and the failure mode it
prevents is not obvious until you look for it.

Every write mints one more physical record tuple, and an \emph{uncommitted} tuple
cannot be discarded (its transaction might still commit) or coalesced with anything
(nothing has superseded it yet, since nothing else can even see it). A naive update
path is simply ``mark the old version dead, append the new one'' on every call ---
which means a single open transaction that writes the same key $N$ times before
committing leaves $N$ physical, uncommitted tuples for that one key, none of them
reclaimable until the transaction resolves. If $N$ grows large relative to a page's
capacity, the split-boundary search in \S\ref{sec:smo} runs out of options entirely:
a page dominated by one key's own version chain has no key boundary to cut at all. A
concrete, realistic trigger for this: a single order transaction that touches the
same inventory row twice (two order lines for the same item) writes that row twice
within one transaction.

The fix is a single equality check performed before falling through to the normal
versioned path:

\begin{lstlisting}
// Self-overwrite fast path: this record was already written
// by *this* transaction -- nobody else can possibly be
// relying on its current content, so mutate it in place.
if leaf_page.version_at(position).insertion_stamp() == stamp {
    leaf_page.set_payload_at(position, payload);
    return (CRUDOperationResult::Updated(stamp.ts_start()), false);
}

// Otherwise: normal versioned update (delete old, push new).
if !leaf_page.version_mut_at(position).delete(stamp) {
    return (CRUDOperationResult::ZeroAffected(KeyAlreadyDeleted), false);
}
let current_len = leaf_page.len();
leaf_page.push_uncommitted(
    RecordPoint::new(key, VersionInfo::new(stamp), payload), current_len);
leaf_page.commit_delta(0, 1);
(CRUDOperationResult::Updated(stamp.ts_start()), true)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Detection} is a single comparison, not a heuristic: a transaction stamp
packs \code{(worker\_id, ts\_start)} and is constant for the \emph{whole}
transaction, so ``was the newest live version of this key written by me, in this
very transaction'' reduces to one equality test. \textbf{Safety} follows directly
from visibility: the found record is only reachable this way if it belongs to the
same transaction, and nothing else can possibly be relying on the current content of
a tuple nobody else can see yet. If the found record belongs to another transaction,
or is already committed, the code falls through to the ordinary
delete-then-append path unchanged, so this is a strict fast path with no change to
external behavior.

The mirror-image case is a delete followed by a reinsert of the same key within one
transaction. A delete must physically retain the predecessor (abort needs to be able
to undelete it), so the first delete/insert pair in a transaction must append a
genuine replacement tuple. But repeating that pair --- delete, reinsert, delete,
reinsert --- would otherwise append another replacement on every cycle. The fix
checks whether the newest record's insertion \emph{and} deletion stamps both belong
to the current transaction, and if so, clears the deletion mark and replaces the
payload in place instead of appending again:

\begin{lstlisting}
if leaf_page.version_at(position).insertion_stamp() == stamp
    && leaf_page.version_at(position).deletion_stamp() == Some(stamp)
{
    leaf_page.version_mut_at(position).undelete();
    leaf_page.set_payload_at(position, payload);
    leaf_page.commit_delta(1, -1);
    return (CRUDOperationResult::Inserted(stamp.ts_start()), false);
}
\end{lstlisting}

Together, these two checks give a clean bound: $N$ updates or delete/reinsert
cycles on one key by one transaction cost at most \emph{one} unresolved replacement
tuple, not $N$. A transaction attempting to extend another transaction's still-open
chain on the same key is simply rejected (first-writer-wins), so this bound holds
under concurrency too, not just within one thread. Once a writer resolves, ordinary
compaction discards the obsolete committed versions, and pages containing several
distinct keys remain freely splittable at a key boundary --- the version-growth
problem this section opened with never gets a chance to occur.

\subsection{Caching resolved roots across a transaction's lifetime}
\label{sec:rootcache}

Finding ``the root as of my snapshot'' for a given table requires a lookup into that
table's root-version index (\S\ref{sec:root}). A transaction that reads or writes the
same table more than once --- the common case for anything beyond a single-statement
operation --- would otherwise repeat that lookup on every call, even though the
answer cannot change: root histories are append-only, and once a snapshot's root has
been resolved, no later publication can change what that specific, already-fixed
snapshot should see.

Each transaction keeps a small inline table of per-table state, one entry per table
it has actually touched, each holding that table's tree handle and (once resolved)
its cached root for this transaction's snapshot:

\begin{lstlisting}
fn read_tree_and_root(&mut self, table: TableId) -> (Arc<Tree>, BlockRef) {
    let slot = self.table_slot(table);
    if let Some(root) = &self.tables[slot].read_root {
        return (self.tables[slot].tree.clone(), root.clone());
    }
    let tree = self.tables[slot].tree.clone();
    let root = tree.retrieve_root_for(self.ts_start);
    self.tables[slot].read_root = Some(root.clone());
    (tree, root)
}
\end{lstlisting}

The first read or write against a table resolves and caches its root; every
subsequent operation against that same table, for the rest of the transaction, reuses
the cached handle instead of re-walking the root index.

\subsection{Inline bookkeeping: \code{SmallVec} instead of a pointer chase}
\label{sec:smallvec}

The table-state list that root caching above lives in --- and, as this subsection
covers, the transaction's write set, the database's own table list, and a
TPC-C-specific write set --- are all the same container type: a \code{SmallVec},
which embeds its element buffer directly inside the surrounding struct up to a fixed
inline capacity, and only falls back to a separate heap allocation if that capacity
is ever exceeded. Below the inline capacity, correctness is unaffected by any of
this --- it behaves exactly like an ordinary growable vector, spilling to the heap
transparently the moment it needs to. What changes is locality: a transaction is
created, used, and dropped by exactly one worker thread, so keeping its own
bookkeeping inline means that thread's repeated touches to its own transaction stay
inside the same handful of cache lines the transaction object itself occupies,
instead of chasing a pointer out to a second, independently allocated buffer for
what is, in the overwhelming majority of cases, a small, fixed-shape piece of state.
This is the same locality principle behind the per-worker slots in
\S\ref{sec:sharding} and the thread-local caches in \S\ref{sec:snapcache}, applied
one level down, to a single transaction's own working set rather than to a
tree-wide array indexed by worker.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The per-table cache} (\S\ref{sec:rootcache}, capacity 8) holds one entry
        per table the transaction has actually touched --- a tree handle and, once
        resolved, the cached root above. Eight entries comfortably covers an
        ordinary multi-table OLTP transaction; an unusually wide one simply spills.
        Because it is inline, checking ``have I already resolved this table's
        root'' is a linear scan over a handful of contiguous entries sitting next
        to the transaction's other fields, not a hash-map lookup and not a chase
        through a second allocation --- for a list this short, that scan is not an
        approximation of a smarter data structure, it \emph{is} the fast path.
  \item \textbf{The write set} (capacity 16) records every key this transaction has
        actually written, in the true order it wrote them, and is exactly what
        abort (\S\ref{sec:abort}, below) walks in reverse. An ordinary transaction's
        writes stay inline for the whole transaction, so both the writes
        themselves and the eventual abort walk over them touch memory local to the
        transaction object rather than a second buffer allocated and then
        immediately torn back down.
  \item \textbf{The database's own table list} (capacity 256) stores every table's
        name and tree handle. Tables are created once, rarely, at database
        setup, so the realistic table count is always far below this capacity in
        practice. The benefit here is subtly different from the two cases above:
        the table list is reached through one shared, atomically published handle
        (\S\ref{sec:sharding}), and a plain growable vector behind that handle
        would need \emph{two} heap indirections to reach a given table --- one to
        reach the vector's own header, a second to follow the header's separate
        buffer pointer to the actual entries. An inline buffer sits inside the
        same allocation as the header, which is itself already inside the shared
        handle's own allocation, so resolving a table costs exactly one
        indirection, not two, for as long as the table count stays under 256.
  \item \textbf{A TPC-C-specific write set} (capacity 24) exists because TPC-C
        writes to two structurally independent tree configurations
        (\S\ref{sec:bigtree}) within one logical transaction, and abort's reverse
        ordering has to reflect the transaction's true, combined write order
        across \emph{both} configurations, not two independently-reversed
        per-configuration lists --- so this write set is deliberately one single
        inline list spanning both, sized generously above this workload's typical
        transaction shape rather than reused verbatim from the general-purpose
        write set above.
\end{itemize}

None of these capacities are load-bearing for correctness --- they were each chosen
to comfortably cover the common case for what uses them, with the ordinary,
non-catastrophic fallback of a real heap allocation on the rare transaction or
table catalog that actually needs more.

\subsection{Abort: reverse order, batched under one latch}
\label{sec:abort}

Reverting a transaction's writes is not one atomic step across every key at once ---
each individual reversal re-exposes that one key to any other concurrent transaction
the instant it completes, before the rest of the abort has finished. This makes the
\emph{order} of reversal a correctness question, not just a performance one:
if an earlier write in the transaction effectively acted as a gate that a later
write depended on (for example, removing a queued work item before updating the
records that item referred to), reverting in forward chronological order would
re-expose that gate \emph{first}, while several of its dependents are still
mid-reversal --- a concurrent reader could observe the gate reopened and race the
transaction's own in-progress rollback of the state it gated. Reverting in reverse
(last write first) avoids this: by the time the gating write's own reversal makes it
visible again, everything the transaction did after it has already been fully
undone.

The write set is walked back to front, and adjacent writes to the \emph{same} key
are collapsed into a single call that reverts all of them in one leaf traversal and
one write latch, rather than one traversal per version:

\begin{lstlisting}
let mut end = self.written.len();
while end != 0 {
    let (slot, key) = self.written[end - 1];
    let mut start = end - 1;
    while start != 0 && self.written[start - 1] == (slot, key) {
        start -= 1;
    }
    self.tables[slot].tree.abort_writes(key, stamp, end - start);
    end = start;
}
\end{lstlisting}

Since the version-growth bound in \S\ref{sec:selfoverwrite} already keeps repeated
writes to one key inside a transaction as a short, physically adjacent run in the
write set, this batching is not a rare case: it is the direct consumer of that
bound, turning ``however many times this transaction touched this key'' into one
latch acquisition instead of one per touch.

A transaction that ends without ever writing anything (a read-only transaction, or
one whose only attempted write returned \code{Conflict}) releases its snapshot
directly, without drawing a commit timestamp or appending a commit-log entry at all
--- there is nothing to make visible and nothing to revert, so both the commit and
abort paths degenerate to the one step that is actually needed.

\subsection{The auto-commit fast path for single-key operations}

Alongside multi-statement transactions, the tree exposes single-key operations that
commit themselves: an auto-committed insert, update, or delete is not a snapshot
transaction with a one-entry write set, it is a shorter protocol tailored to exactly
one key. Its write path, for an insert:

\begin{lstlisting}
let leaf_guard = self.traversal_write_olc(key);
// ... find newest version, check visibility, decide ...
let stamp = self.wal_start_commit(|_| CRUDOperation::Insert(key, payload.clone()));
leaf_page.push_uncommitted(RecordPoint::new(key, VersionInfo::new(stamp), payload), len);
leaf_page.commit_delta(1, 0);

// Commit while the leaf is still write-locked. OLC readers cannot
// validate the new prefix until the guard is released, so the version
// is already in OSIC's commit log at the first instant it can be
// observed successfully.
let ts_commit = self.commit_tx(stamp.worker_id());
drop(leaf_guard);
self.wal_log_commit(stamp, ts_commit);
\end{lstlisting}

The order of the last three steps is the entire point: the commit-log append happens
\emph{before} the leaf guard is released, not after. Since a lock-free reader cannot
successfully validate a leaf while it is write-locked, the earliest possible instant
any reader could observe the new record is strictly after the commit-log entry
already exists --- closing what would otherwise be a window where a reader sees a
published record whose commit has not yet landed. Releasing the WAL's durability
record is left for afterward, deliberately: visibility and durability are separate
guarantees here (\S\ref{sec:osic}), so the caller is never blocked on disk I/O to get
a result back.

An auto-committed write also does not register a full OSIC snapshot the way a
transaction's \code{begin} does: it draws a start timestamp for the record's own
stamp and a commit timestamp for the commit log, but never publishes the start
timestamp as a long-lived, GC-visible reader --- it does not retain a root, a page,
or a timestamp after the call returns, so there is nothing later that a full
snapshot registration would need to protect. Protection during the traversal itself
comes from a lighter-weight reclamation pin rather than a full snapshot, sized to
exactly the guarantee this shorter-lived operation actually needs.

A closely related fast path lets a caller atomically read-and-patch a single field
of an existing row while holding the very same leaf write latch used for the write
itself, deriving the replacement payload from the current one in one guarded step ---
so a single-field update never has to materialize, mutate, and write back an entire
row, and never risks losing a concurrent updater's change to a different field of
the same row, because both are serialized by the one latch the whole operation runs
under.

\section{Garbage collection: sharded, lock-free reclamation}
\label{sec:gc}

\subsection{Liveness driven directly off per-worker state}

Deciding whether a retired page can be reused reduces to one question: is there any
live reader whose snapshot might still need it? Rather than maintaining a separate
index of active transactions to answer this, liveness is read directly off the same
per-worker snapshot slots described in \S\ref{sec:sharding} --- the oldest currently
published snapshot across all workers is the answer, computed with a plain scan over
a small, fixed number of per-worker slots rather than a query into a second,
independently-maintained structure that could disagree with the first.

\subsection{Own-shard-first reclaim with round-robin stealing}

Retired pages are indexed in one shard per worker rather than one shared structure:
a page a worker retires is registered only into that worker's own shard, and a
worker looking for a page to reuse checks its own shard first, falling back to a
shared round-robin cursor across other shards only when its own is empty. Since a
reclaiming worker only needs \emph{a} reusable page, not the globally oldest one,
trading strict global reuse order for per-worker insertion locality is a clean
win: the common case touches only the calling worker's own shard and its own cache
lines, and the fallback path only runs when that shard genuinely has nothing to
offer.

\subsection{Historical routing keeps old readers off the hot reclaim path}

An older reader is never the reason a rebuild has to copy a record forward into a
replacement page: instead, that reader continues to route through the retired
source block via the historical root/version chain until it finishes, at which
point the block becomes reusable on its own. This keeps the decision ``does this
specific replacement page need to retain this record'' local to what
structurally-current readers and possible aborts actually need, rather than coupled
to whatever the globally oldest reader anywhere happens to be doing.

\section{Sharding as a system-wide pattern}
\label{sec:sharding}

Sharding, in this codebase, is not horizontal key-range partitioning of one index.
It is the same move applied four times: take a piece of hot, shared bookkeeping
state and partition it by worker (or, in one case, by table) so that unrelated
threads stop contending on the same cache lines.

\subsection{Tables as shards}

Each logical table gets its own physically separate tree, its own root index, and
its own dead-block tracker, rather than several tables being multiplexed into one
shared tree. Concurrent transactions touching different tables therefore never
contend on one tree's root or internal-page latches, or on one shared dead-block
index. The table list itself is published behind a lock-free read-copy-update
handle: readers resolve a table with one atomic load and no lock at all, and adding
a new table --- a rare, cold-path operation --- builds a new table list and swaps it
in without ever blocking a concurrent reader. What must stay global for
cross-table snapshot isolation --- the shared clock, commit-log pruning, and
snapshot liveness --- lives in one shared, per-database context rather than being
duplicated per table.

\subsection{Cache-padded, per-worker atomic slots}

Each worker's currently published snapshot, its transaction-nesting depth, and a
conservative in-flight registration bound each live in their own array, one slot per
worker, written only by that worker and read with a simple scan by anyone computing
the oldest live snapshot. This directly replaced an earlier design built around
shared structures --- a shared ordered map of active snapshots, and a single shared
counter tracking in-flight registrations --- both of which turned into serialization
points precisely because every worker had to touch the same shared location on
every registration and every liveness check.

Because adjacent workers' slots in the same array can otherwise share one 64-byte
cache line, each of these three per-worker arrays pads every entry out to its own
cache line. Without that padding, one worker updating its own logically-independent
slot would still invalidate a neighboring worker's cached copy of \emph{their}
slot purely because the two happen to share a line --- a classic false-sharing cost
that grows directly with core count, and one that a purely single-writer-per-slot
design can otherwise look immune to on paper while still paying for it in practice.

\section{Per-thread identity and the snapshot cache}
\label{sec:snapcache}

This section covers, in detail, how a thread knows who it is on a given tree and
how visibility checks against \emph{other} workers are memoized --- the mechanism
that the sharding pattern above (\S\ref{sec:sharding}) depends on having in the
first place.

\subsection{Thread-local worker assignment}

A worker identity is a small integer, handed out once per (tree, thread) from a
tree-owned counter and never returned, matching a model of a fixed pool of
long-lived worker threads rather than one identity per short-lived task. Each thread
remembers the identities it has already been given in thread-local storage, keyed
not by the tree's address but by a process-wide unique id assigned to the tree at
construction --- necessary because a dropped tree's memory can be reused by an
unrelated later tree, and a thread that cached an identity for the old occupant
must not silently reuse it for the new one.

The cache itself keeps exactly two most-recently-used entries inline, in a plain
copyable cell with no runtime borrow-checking, plus a lazily allocated overflow list
for the rare case of a thread touching more than two trees. Two inline slots (not
one, and not a larger searchable array) are a deliberate match to the actual access
pattern: real deployments create exactly one tree that lives for the process's whole
lifetime, so the common case is a single, unchanging comparison against the primary
slot; a thread that occasionally alternates between two trees still gets a cheap
hit on the second slot; and only a thread that genuinely cycles through three or
more trees pays for the overflow path at all. A larger fixed-size searchable array
was tried first and discarded specifically because it optimized for a
test-harness convenience (many short-lived trees per process, never interleaved)
at the cost of complexity on every production lookup, where the actual shape of
real usage is one or two long-lived trees.

\subsection{The snapshot cache: structure-of-arrays visibility memoization}

Visibility --- ``is this write, made by some other worker, visible to my
snapshot'' --- is checked by comparing a reader's start timestamp against a lower
commit bound (LCB) computed from the writing worker's own commit log. That LCB
computation involves a lock and a search, so recomputing it on every single record a
reader touches would be wasteful whenever the same foreign worker's writes keep
coming up against the same reader snapshot --- exactly the common case inside one
range scan or one busy transaction.

Each worker keeps its own cache, one entry per \emph{other} worker it might need a
visibility answer about, laid out as two parallel arrays rather than one array of
pairs:

\begin{lstlisting}
pub struct SnapshotCache {
    // Structure-of-arrays: snapshot identity and LCB values are scanned or
    // refreshed independently and no longer force 16-byte tuple traffic.
    snapshot_versions: Box<[Version]>,
    lcb: Box<[Version]>,
}
\end{lstlisting}

The visibility check itself reads only what it needs, in an order chosen so the
common cases touch the fewest cache lines:

\begin{lstlisting}
if stamp.is_invalid() { return false; }              // aborted: never visible
if stamp.worker_id() == reader_worker {
    return stamp.ts_start() <= reader_ts_start;       // own writes: direct compare
}
let index = stamp.worker_id() as usize;
if cache.lcb[index] > stamp.ts_start() {
    return true;                                      // cache hit: touches `lcb` only
}
if cache.snapshot_versions[index] < reader_ts_start {
    cache.lcb[index] = commit_logs[index].lcb(reader_ts_start);
    cache.snapshot_versions[index] = reader_ts_start;  // refresh: locked, real query
}
cache.lcb[index] > stamp.ts_start()
\end{lstlisting}

An aborted write is rejected first, deliberately ahead of the same-worker fast path
--- otherwise that fast path would keep an aborted write visible to its own writer
forever, since it never consults the commit log at all and an uncommitted write's
absence from that log would never get a chance to matter. A worker's own writes are
resolved with one direct comparison, no cache lookup needed, and deliberately
support reading an intentionally historical snapshot (a start timestamp \emph{older}
than the write itself), not just ``my current view.'' For a foreign worker, the
common case --- this worker's cached LCB for that foreign worker is already known to
exceed the write's timestamp --- touches only the \code{lcb} array and returns
immediately; only a genuine cache miss touches \code{snapshot\_versions} and pays
for a real, lock-protected query into that worker's commit log. Because the two
streams are separate arrays rather than one array of pairs, a hit never loads the
\code{snapshot\_versions} entry it does not need, and a miss's refresh writes both
without disturbing any other worker's entries in either array.

This cache is created fresh for a tree's actual configured worker budget, not against
any fixed compile-time bound: sizing is a runtime parameter of the tree, one boxed
slice per stream, sized once when the cache is created. There is no hard-coded
maximum-worker ceiling baked into the type itself --- a tree configured for more
workers simply allocates larger boxed slices for its per-thread caches, and one
configured for fewer wastes nothing on unused capacity.

Like the worker-identity cache above, this per-tree cache lives in thread-local
storage, keyed the same way, with the same two-inline-slots-plus-overflow shape:
the paper's own ``thread-local snapshot cache'' idea is realized literally, using
actual thread-local storage rather than a shared array indexed by worker id, so that
no thread ever \emph{could} touch another worker's cache by mistake, not merely a
convention that every call site happens to follow.

\section{OSIC and the write-ahead log}
\label{sec:osic}

\subsection{Instant commit, lazily hardened}
\label{sec:instantcommit}

Every transaction draws its start and commit timestamps from one global,
monotonically increasing counter. Each worker keeps its own append-only commit log;
committing means drawing a commit timestamp and pushing it onto that log under a
short lock --- an in-memory step, with nothing on the commit path waiting on disk
I/O. Durability catches up separately and in batches: writes and commit markers are
appended to a background writer, which periodically batches everything accumulated
so far into one write-and-sync call and advances a published ``hardened'' watermark
that a caller can poll explicitly if it specifically needs a durability guarantee.
Recovery replays only writes that have a matching commit marker, ordered by commit
time rather than log order, since commit order can differ from log order across
concurrent workers.

\subsection{Sharding the write channel}

A single shared channel from every committing worker to one background flush
thread is itself a piece of shared, contended state, in exactly the sense
\S\ref{sec:sharding} describes: many independent writers funnel into the same
channel and the same background thread. Splitting the channel into several
independent (channel, flush-thread) shards, keyed by worker identity, removes that
contention the same way the other sharding optimizations do --- each shard still
writes through one shared file handle, so the durable format and recovery logic are
unaffected, but the hot enqueue path no longer funnels every worker through the same
lock-free structure.

\subsection{Ticket-less logging and exact framing sizes}

Two further, purely allocation-side costs were found and removed once the
contention above was no longer dominant. First, every log call built a fresh
acknowledgment channel to hand back a ``flush ticket'' that ordinary production
callers never actually used --- logging methods now default to a ticket-less path
with no channel built at all, with an explicit ticketed variant kept only for the
handful of callers that genuinely need to wait on one specific record. Second, every
framing buffer's capacity hint had been sized for a small fixed-width test payload,
forcing a real row's much larger encoding to grow and reallocate the buffer several
times during a single write; each payload type now reports its own exact encoded
size up front, so the buffer for a real row is sized once, correctly, on the first
allocation.

\section{Range scans and analytical terminals}
\label{sec:scans}

\subsection{Cursor-based internal routing}

An internal page is append/version ordered, not key ordered: structural
modifications append replacement child entries while retaining historical entries
that older snapshots still need to route through. A range scan therefore cannot
walk physical slots left to right, or sort every snapshot-matching slot, without
risking overlapping, superseded entries being included alongside their
replacements. Instead, at each level the scan searches entries in reverse append
order and selects the newest snapshot-visible interval containing the current scan
cursor, descends, and after consuming a leaf advances the cursor one key past that
leaf's boundary and reroutes from the retained parent --- the same selection rule as
a point lookup, allocating no child lists or deduplication structures at any point.
An exact full-domain scan is detected once, before the leaf loop begins, and uses a
loop that skips the two range-bound comparisons every bounded scan still needs per
record, since a full-domain scan's range trivially contains every key it could ever
see.

\subsection{Zero-copy streaming terminals}

Alongside the ordinary iterator (which materializes each visible record into an
owned result and clones its payload handle, for callers who need results to outlive
the scan), a family of terminal operations --- for-each, fold, count, and a
fallible early-exit variant --- calls a visitor directly with a borrowed key and
payload reference, never constructing an owned result object or cloning a payload
handle at all. An analytical consumer that only ever aggregates (sums a column,
counts matching rows) never needed an owned copy in the first place; these
terminals let it not pay for one.

\textbf{Measured:} on a 200,000-row full scan, the streaming terminal ran
1.165$\times$--1.173$\times$ faster than the materialized iterator ($\sim$16.8\%;
28.8--29.3\,M rows/s vs.\ 24.7--25.1\,M rows/s). A specialized counting terminal,
which additionally skips constructing even a borrowed-result wrapper, reached
$\sim$49--53\,M rows/s on the same kind of full scan.

\subsection{Testing the cheap predicate first}

Every physical record in a leaf is filtered by two predicates: is it within the
requested key range, and is it visible to the reader's snapshot. The visibility
check can require up to two lookups into a foreign worker's commit log (one for the
insertion stamp, one for the deletion stamp, via the snapshot cache in
\S\ref{sec:snapcache}); the range check is one or two integer comparisons. Ordering
the range check first means an out-of-range record, however many superseded
versions of other keys it happens to share a leaf with, is rejected before ever
touching the more expensive check --- a direct instance of the same ``reject
cheaply before paying for the expensive check'' pattern the leaf's live/validity
mask (\S\ref{sec:physical}) already applies one level earlier, applied here to the
scan's own per-record filter instead of to a whole slot. A full-table scan gets no
benefit from this reordering (its range trivially contains everything), but a
narrow scan sharing a leaf with many keys outside its own range benefits directly,
in proportion to how many of that leaf's records the range check alone can already
reject.

\subsection{Monomorphizing the visibility check}

The reordering above only helps records the range check can already reject; every
record actually within range still pays for the visibility check itself, and that
check previously took its ``is this visible'' predicate as a type-erased callback ---
an indirect call the compiler cannot inline, paid on every insertion and deletion
stamp of every in-range record. The range-scan iterator's own visibility check now
builds a concrete, statically-typed closure once per refill (covering every leaf that
one refill call visits, not rebuilding it per leaf), so the compiler can inline the
whole check directly into the scan loop rather than dispatching through a function
pointer for every single record.

\section{Workload and benchmark-harness optimizations}
\label{sec:workload}

A number of optimizations sit in the benchmark and workload-generation code itself
rather than in the storage engine proper --- worth including in full, since an
engine-side optimization measured against a benchmark that is itself spending
unrelated CPU time on request generation will systematically understate its own
effect.

\subsection{Fast, thread-local randomness}

The obvious choice for a random-number generator, \code{rand}'s default, is
cryptographically secure (ChaCha-backed) --- unnecessary strength for load-generator
data that never needs to be unpredictable, and expensive enough that it measured as
\textbf{up to $\sim$14\% of all CPU cycles} in a read-heavy benchmark, spent purely on
picking which key to touch next. The fix follows the same shape everywhere it is
applied: seed a fast, non-cryptographic generator once per thread from the secure
generator, then draw from that thread-local instance for every subsequent request ---
paying the expensive seeding cost exactly once per thread's lifetime, not once per
operation:

\begin{lstlisting}
thread_local! {
    static FAST_RNG: RefCell<SmallRng> = RefCell::new(rand::make_rng());
}
fn with_fast_rng<R>(f: impl FnOnce(&mut SmallRng) -> R) -> R {
    FAST_RNG.with(|rng| f(&mut rng.borrow_mut()))
}
\end{lstlisting}

The key-value benchmark driver uses \code{SmallRng} (Xoshiro256++); the
order-processing benchmark driver uses \code{fastrand}'s \code{Rng} (WyRand) instead
--- two different concrete generators for two different drivers, but the identical
pattern: a thread owns its own generator exclusively, with no shared state and no
lock, the same principle behind every per-worker cache elsewhere in this document
(\S\ref{sec:sharding}, \S\ref{sec:snapcache}).

\subsection{The alias-table Zipf sampler}

A Zipfian-skewed workload needs to draw a ``how popular is this key'' rank from a
skewed distribution on every single request. Computed naively, that draw needs a
floating-point power evaluation per request --- itself a real cost at millions of
requests per second. Walker's alias method avoids this by doing all of the expensive
work exactly once, at setup: it builds a small table of paired probabilities and
alternate indices in $O(n)$ time (the only place a power computation ever runs), and
after that, sampling is $O(1)$ --- one random index draw, one random comparison, and
a table lookup, with no floating-point power anywhere in the timed request path:

\begin{lstlisting}
fn sample(&self, rng: &mut SmallRng) -> u64 {
    let column = rng.random_range(0..self.probability.len());
    let index = if rng.random::<f32>() < self.probability[column] {
        column
    } else {
        self.alias[column] as usize
    };
    index as u64 + 1
}
\end{lstlisting}

The table itself is immutable after construction, so every thread samples from the
same one without needing a lock or a per-thread copy --- only the source of
randomness driving the two draws above is thread-local, per the previous subsection.

\subsection{Rank scrambling: a realism fix, not a speedup}

One subtlety is easy to get wrong here. Left alone, a Zipf sample's rank is used
directly as the key: rank 1 (the most popular draw) \emph{is} key 1, rank 2 is key
2, and so on for the whole run. For this specific tree, that means the entire hot
set of a Zipfian workload sits in the lowest few thousand keys for the run's whole
duration --- physically, the same handful of leftmost leaf pages, every time. That
is a windfall this engine did not earn: a page-based buffer-pool engine being
compared against in the same benchmark has to keep a hot set that is \emph{spread
across the full key range} warm under a fixed memory budget, which is a
meaningfully harder problem than keeping a few always-resident leftmost pages warm.
Comparing raw throughput under that asymmetry would not be comparing the two
engines' actual concurrency/caching behavior at all.

The fix hashes the drawn rank through an FNV-1 hash before treating it as a key,
matching the scrambling real Zipfian workload generators apply for exactly this
reason. This preserves the skew --- rank 1 is still drawn most often, and still
resolves to one fixed key for the whole run --- while spreading \emph{which}
physical keys are hot across the entire key range instead of clustering them at the
bottom of it. This is deliberately \emph{not} applied to a ``read the most recently
inserted keys'' access pattern, whose entire point is bias toward physically recent
keys --- scrambling that would defeat the pattern it is meant to model. Unlike every
other optimization in this document, this one does not make the engine faster: it
makes the benchmark honest about what it is measuring.

\section{Case study: trading contention for scan cost}
\label{sec:bigtree}

This section follows one optimization end to end because it is the clearest example
in the system of a design whose two extremes are each bounded by a genuinely
different, mechanistic cost --- worth understanding as a whole rather than as a
single tuning knob.

\subsection{The problem: two tiny tables dominate contention}

Attributing where concurrent transactions spend time waiting to retry finds that two
of the smallest tables in a typical order-processing schema --- tables with a
handful of rows per warehouse-like partition --- account for the overwhelming
majority of all such retries, despite being tiny by row count. The reason is write
volume, not size: every order touches them, their physical version chains grow
quickly, and a leaf's compaction threshold is a hard, fixed fraction of its
capacity, so these two tables cross that threshold and force a page-locking
compaction far more often, per row, than any other table in the schema.

\subsection{The fix: an independent, tunable leaf capacity}

These two tables are given their own tree configuration, independent of every other
table's leaf capacity, chosen from a small family of page-exact sizes rather than
one hand-picked number. Giving \emph{only} these two tables a larger capacity avoids
paying the memory and alignment cost of a larger leaf on every one of the schema's
other tables, which were never the source of the contention in the first place.

\subsection{Why the trade-off has a floor and a ceiling}

The upper end of this trade-off is unavoidable, not a tuning oversight: a leaf's
compaction threshold is also exactly when its accumulated dead, superseded versions
get reclaimed, and a range scan over that table has to examine a leaf's entire
physical record array, live and dead alike. A larger capacity therefore means more
accumulated garbage sits in the leaf between compactions, and every scan over that
table pays for wading through it --- there is no way to independently choose ``avoid
frequent compactions'' and ``keep scan-time garbage bounded'' in this design; they
are the same knob, turned in opposite directions.

The lower end has a genuine floor for a structural reason: every split pushes two
fresh entries into a parent rather than overwriting one, so a freshly split node
needs enough raw capacity to accept at least one further push before it, too, needs
to split --- push a capacity small enough and a freshly split node can find itself
already back at its own overflow threshold with no room to accept even the very
next write, regardless of how much concurrency is involved. This makes the smallest
usable capacity a property of the split algorithm itself, not merely something that
happens to perform poorly if chosen too small.

\subsection{The measured middle ground}

The default choice sits at the point where a scan over these two tables' rows
recovers essentially full throughput relative to an unmodified leaf, while still
capturing most of the achievable reduction in retry contention; larger capacities
trade further contention headroom for scan cost that grows faster than the
contention benefit does, once the scan has to age past the first few seconds of
accumulated garbage. This is a genuinely explicit, chosen point on a continuum, not
a default that happens to work: reaching for a larger capacity is a deliberate
choice for a deployment whose read pattern can tolerate slower scans on these two
tables in exchange for less contention on their writes, not a strictly better
setting.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Taken together, the optimizations in this document are variations on a small set of
ideas, applied at every layer of the system: lay data out so a cheap check can reject
most of it before a more expensive one runs (the leaf's live mask, the internal
page's live mask, range-before-visibility ordering); give every lock-free reader
something it can validate without ever taking a lock (optimistic lock coupling, the
snapshot cache); and partition shared bookkeeping along the one axis --- worker
identity --- that lets each partition be touched by only the thread that owns it
(per-worker snapshot slots, sharded reclamation, sharded write channels). The
version-growth bound in \S\ref{sec:selfoverwrite} and the root-caching and
batched-abort optimizations in \S\ref{sec:tx} are the same idea again, one layer up:
notice what a transaction is guaranteed never to need to repeat, and stop paying for
repeating it.
